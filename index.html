Olympic-Level Execution

TECHNIQUE LIBRARY

Complete breakdowns of every stroke, phase, drill, and race element — with elite benchmarks, exact numbers, and the physiology behind every movement.

ENTRY & HIGH ELBOW CATCH

▾

Hand enters narrow (inside shoulder line) at 10–15° downward. The high elbow catch is the single highest-ROI skill in swimming. Elbow rises and flares outward while wrist presses down, creating a large vertical forearm surface against still water.

🎯 Cue: "Reach around a barrel." Elbow sets high and wide before the hand moves back at all.
Entry angle
10–15° below horizontal.
Elbow height
Always above wrist through catch.
Catch depth
Forearm vertical, hand below the elbow — about 40–50 cm deep at the catch and ~55 cm at its deepest mid-pull (what the 3D lab shows).
Elite catch
From entry to a locked catch takes about 0.3–0.45 s at race rate (the lab shows ~0.45 s at 48 cycles/min) — quick, but never rushed past the extension.

PULL PATH & HAND ACCELERATION

▾

The hand path is nearly straight back under the body — the slight S you see on film comes from the body roll, not from sweeping the hand. Hand accelerates throughout — slowest at catch, fastest at exit. The hand must push all the way past the hip — thumb brushing the thigh.

🎯 Cue: "Slow in, fast out." The catch should feel controlled. The finish should feel like a snap.

BODY ROTATION & HEAD POSITION

▾

Shoulder roll: about 45–60° each side at distance pace, less (30–40°) in the 50/100 where turnover is higher; the hips roll roughly half as much. Head position: hairline at the surface, eyes at the pool floor. Even a 5 cm head lift forces the hips down.

6-BEAT KICK MECHANICS

▾

The kick supplies up to ~30% of propulsion in sprint freestyle (≈10–15% at distance pace). The kick originates at the hip — knee bends only 30–40° on the downbeat. Ankle plantarflexion is the biggest limiter for most swimmers.

🎯 Cue: "Kick from your hip pocket, not your knee."

GOLF SCORE SYSTEM

▾

Golf Score = 50 m split (seconds) + stroke count per 50 m (count each arm in free and back; one stroke per cycle in fly and breast). Lower is always better. Elite benchmarks (a 50 at 100-pace effort, 50 m pool): Below 60 = world class. 60–66 = senior national. 67–74 = competitive club. Fly and breast read about 15 lower on this scale, so compare those against your own trend.

BODY POSITION & DUCK DRILL

▾

Correct position: hips at the surface, ears slightly submerged, eyes up toward the ceiling with the chin neutral (look at the flags, not at your feet — a chin-tuck lifts the head, drops the hips and loads the neck). Even a small hip drop adds frontal area and the drag climbs with it — at race speed a few centimeters costs measurable time. The Duck drill is that position without the arms: backstroke kick with the arms at your sides, chin neutral and hips riding high, so the body sits on the surface the way a duck does — kick a length this way before adding the arms, then keep exactly the same head and hip position once you do.

🎯 Cue: "Squeeze a coin between your glutes, belly button to the ceiling."

PINKY ENTRY & 2-2-2 DRILL

▾

Entry is pinky-first, hand rotated so the palm faces out — pinky down, thumb up. Arm enters directly above the shoulder. The 2-2-2 drill (2 right strokes, 2 left strokes, 2 full) isolates each side.

2K1 SCULL — THE CORE DRILL

▾

2K1 (two kicks, one stroke) is the foundational breaststroke drill. Doubling the kick slows tempo dramatically — forcing the swimmer to feel the glide, catch, and full out-scull before pulling.

🎯 Progression: Deep 2K1 scull → Shallow 2K1 scull → Flat rhythm → Full speed.

BREASTSTROKE KICK — 60–70% OF PROPULSION

▾

Heels draw up narrow — not wide. Feet evert into "duck feet" before sweeping in a circular arc and finishing together in streamline.

🎯 Cue: "Heels narrow, toes wide, sweep out-around-together."

UNDULATION — THE WAVE FROM CHEST TO FEET

▾

Butterfly is wave-driven, not arm-driven. The movement: chest presses DOWN → hips rise → feet drive DOWN. Hip amplitude: about 10–20 cm — the wave grows toward the feet (toes 45–60 cm).

🎯 Cue: "Chest DOWN first — everything else follows."

2-2-2 DRILL & ONE-ARM FLY

▾

2-2-2 drill: 2 right-arm strokes, 2 left-arm strokes, 2 full butterfly strokes. Isolates each side's pull pattern and makes bilateral asymmetries obvious immediately.

One-arm fly: the full body wave and both kicks with one arm stroking and the other extended in front (breathe to the side or to the front), 25 m on each arm — it isolates the kick timing against the pull and shows which side's catch collapses.

UNDERWATER DOLPHIN KICK — THE 5TH STROKE

▾

Underwater dolphin kick is the fastest way a swimmer can propel themselves — quicker than any surface stroke, second only to the push-off itself. At elite level, UW velocity exceeds surface swimming speed. Elite programs feature UW 15 m work in nearly every session.

🎯 Every rep is a race simulation. Count kicks per 15 m weekly.
Streamline
Hands stacked dominant-on-top, arms past ears, core rigid.
Kick amplitude
About 45–55 cm toe-to-toe at the feet (the hips move only ~15 cm). Hip-driven.
Swim depth
0.5–0.6 m below surface.
Elite rate
About 1.9 Hz on average at race speed (≈115 kicks/min), with the fastest sprinters at 2.0–2.5 Hz (120–150). Rate, not amplitude, is what rises with speed.

FREESTYLE FLIP TURN

▾

Elite push-off velocities reach ~2.5–3.0 m/s and decay through the glide. Every 0.1 s lost at the wall costs far more metabolically to recover. Approach at full speed and never look for the wall. Start the somersault as your head crosses the T — its cross line is 2 m from the wall — so the last stroke and tuck bring you round about a body length out. Drive into tight streamline with ≥5 m UW dolphin kick off every wall. The 3D lab's underwater dolphin kick shows the streamline and the first kicks phase by phase.

BREASTSTROKE PULLOUT

▾

The breaststroke pullout is the fastest point in the stroke. Legal sequence: push off → streamline glide → one full underwater pull to the thighs (with one optional dolphin kick) → recover the arms → one breaststroke kick → breakout as the head surfaces. Begin the pull the moment you sense velocity starting to drop.

GRAB START & TRACK START MECHANICS

▾

Track start (one foot back) is now dominant at elite level; the grab start (both feet at the front edge, hands gripping the block) is the older individual start — a stronger but slower push, now rare in elite racing. Relay takeovers use a different start again: the step-through (swing) start, an arm swing from the back of the block timed to the incoming swimmer's touch. Reaction time target: under 0.65 s. Takeoff is near horizontal — the block height gives the flight.

Reaction time
Fast-elite: ~0.60–0.64 s; most swimmers are slower — chase clean, not just fast.
Takeoff angle
Near horizontal — about 0–10° above the horizontal; the 0.5–0.75 m block height gives the flight.
Entry angle
About 30–40° to the surface — hands, head, hips and feet through one hole.
UW distance
Elite: 10–15 m before breakout.

IM ORDER & TRANSITION STRATEGY

▾

IM order: Butterfly → Backstroke → Breaststroke → Freestyle. Each transition is a critical moment where drag and tempo change dramatically.

Fly pacing
Go out controlled — 200 IM is not a 50 fly sprint.
Breast leg
The race decider. If you've gone out too hard, breaststroke is where you pay.
Elite split
200 IM — fly ~22%, back ~25%, breast ~28–29%, free ~24–25% of race time: breaststroke is the slowest leg, fly (with the dive) the fastest. Full patterns in the Race Planner's split reference.

Read the HR numbers as a 10-second count, not bpm. Stop, find your pulse, count for 10 seconds — "HR 24–28" means 24–28 beats in that 10 seconds (roughly 144–168 bpm). Lactate values are typical ranges from the literature, not measurements of you.
Zone 1

RECOVERY

Lactate: below 2.0 mmol/L · HR: under 24 on the 10-second count · Easy enough to hold a full conversation. Warm-up, cool-down, recovery between hard sets — and a large share of base volume: elite programs log about 40% of their meters here (Hellard et al. 2019). Going too hard in what should be Zone 1 is the most common way swimmers blunt the next quality set.

Zone 2 — AeT

AEROBIC THRESHOLD

Lactate: 2.0–3.0 mmol/L · HR: 24–28 on the 10-second count · Zones 1–2 together carry 80%+ of total training volume. Builds mitochondrial density, oxidative enzyme capacity, cardiac output.

Zone 3

THRESHOLD

Lactate: 3.0–5.0 mmol/L · HR: 28–31 on the 10-second count · Three-to-four-word sentences at most. Typically introduced around Week 4 of a season and expanded progressively through Meso II.

Zone 4–5

VO₂ MAX & SPRINT

Zone 4: 5.0–8.0 mmol/L · HR 31–33 on the 10-second count · 3–8 min intervals · 48–72 h recovery. Zone 5: sprint / anaerobic — two kinds. Alactic speed: 10–30 s efforts on full rest; lactate stays low and HR is not the guide. Lactate production: 50–100 m maximal efforts (8.0+ mmol/L, HR near max), 2–5 min rest, 1–2× a week at most.

NUTRITION & FUELING

Pre-AM Session (60–90 min before)

MORNING FUEL

  • 1–2 slices white toast + jam (fast carbs)
  • Banana or 200 ml orange juice
  • 250 ml water on waking
  • Avoid high-fat, high-fiber foods
Recovery Window

POST-SESSION

  • Within 30 min: ~1 g/kg carbs (45–60 g for a 45–60 kg swimmer, 60–80 g at 60–80 kg) + 0.3 g/kg protein (15–25 g)
  • Chocolate milk, Greek yogurt + banana
  • 1–1.5 L water over the next 2 hours
Pre-PM Session (2–3 h before)

AFTERNOON FUEL

  • 80–100 g carbs, 30 g protein, low fat
  • Chicken + rice + vegetables
  • 500 ml water in the 2 hours before

CARBOHYDRATE TARGETS

  • Base training: 6–7 g/kg bodyweight
  • Double days: 8–10 g/kg
  • Taper weeks: 5–6 g/kg as volume drops (weight stays stable)
  • Before a meet: 8–10 g/kg on the day before racing (start 2 days out for a multi-day meet)

PROTEIN TARGETS

  • Total daily: 1.6–2.2 g/kg
  • Per meal: 20–40 g
  • Post-session priority: within 30 min
  • Pre-bed: casein-rich foods
Race day nutrition is PRACTICE nutrition. Never try anything new on race day.
Night Before

CARB LOADING

  • High-carb dinner: pasta, rice, bread
  • Low fiber, low fat
  • 8–10 g/kg over the day
Morning of Race

PRE-MEET MEAL

  • 3–4 h before: 1–2 g/kg carbs
  • 600 ml water with breakfast
  • Caffeine (adult athletes): 3 mg/kg 60 min before — not advised for junior/adolescent swimmers
Between Events

INTRA-MEET

  • 30–60 g simple carbs every 60–90 min
  • 250–350 ml water every 30–45 min
  • Avoid heavy food within 90 min of any event

THE 30-MINUTE RECOVERY WINDOW

Glycogen resynthesis is fastest in the first 30 minutes after training (the rate stays high to about 45). Target: ~1 g/kg carbs + 0.3 g/kg protein — the same dose as the Post-Session card under Daily Fueling. Best options: chocolate milk, rice + protein, yogurt + banana.

Even 2% dehydration measurably reduces performance. Swimmers underestimate fluid loss — you still sweat in the pool, typically 0.3–0.6 L per hour, and never notice it.

DAILY HYDRATION

  • Baseline: 35–45 ml per kg bodyweight per day
  • Add 400–600 ml per hour of training
  • Urine target: pale yellow
  • Electrolytes recommended on 90+ min sessions

Compete at Your Best

RACE STRATEGY

MEET DAY TIMELINE

  • T−3 h: Pre-meet meal. Carb-heavy, familiar, low fiber/fat.
  • T−75 min: Begin warm-up. Never skip.
  • T−30 min: Complete warm-up. Dry off, stay warm, hydrate.
  • T−10 min: Behind the blocks. Controlled breathing.

BEHIND THE BLOCKS

  • Focus only on what you control: first 5 strokes, your turn, your pace
  • Box breathing: 4 in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold
  • One or two focus cues only — more than that is too many (see Mental › Focus & Cues)
  • Never watch your main rivals warm up

COMPETITION WARM-UP PROTOCOL

  • 400–600 m easy: HR below 22 (easy end of Zone 1). No rushing.
  • 4×50 kick: Fins, easy to moderate.
  • 4×50 drills: Race-stroke drills. One technical cue.
  • 4×25 builds: Gradually build to 85% on final 25.
  • 2×15 m sprint: Full race pace from a push.
  • 200 m easy cool-down: Drop HR before the race.
Total: about 1,130–1,330 m. Anything past ~1,800 m is a training set before your race.

PACING SCIENCE

100 m: the first 50 is normally faster than the second — by about 4% of the race time in free and back (≈2 s at 50 s, 2.8 s at 1:10) and about 6% in fly and breast (3–4 s) — the dive and the underwater do that for you, so the job is to minimize the fade, not to negative split. A freestyle second 50 more than ~10% slower than the first (the normal pattern is ~8%) means you went out too hard.

200 m: the second 100 is normally 2–4% (2–3 s) slower than the first in every elite final — about 3% in freestyle, 4% in backstroke — so the target is to hold the third 50 at the pace of the second; butterfly and breaststroke 200s fade more — the second 100 is typically 5–6% slower in breaststroke and 8–10% slower in butterfly, so holding the third 50 is a stretch target there.

400 m and up: even 100s, with the lift coming at the end. The championship-final split patterns behind these targets are in the Race Planner's split reference.

TAPER & RECOVERY

SPRINT TAPER (14–21 days)

  • Week 3 before: volume down 20–30%, speed and power work kept
  • Week 2 before: volume down 40–50%, race-pace and power touches in every session
  • Week 1 before: volume down 60–70%, short fast work, full rest
  • Day before: 20–30 min easy + 4–6×25 race-pace
  • Avoid long rest — 7+ days completely off makes sprinters feel slow

DISTANCE TAPER (7–14 days)

  • Week 2 before: volume down 20–30%, keep one solid aerobic set
  • Week 1 before: volume down 40–50%, keep the daily aerobic rhythm and race-pace 100s/200s
  • Day before: 30–40 min easy with a few builds
  • Trust the process — feeling sluggish mid-taper is normal
Why sprinters taper longer. Sprinters carry more muscle and need longer to freshen; distance swimmers lose aerobic feel with too little volume, so their taper is shorter and shallower.

The Psychological Edge

MENTAL PERFORMANCE

VISUALIZATION — MENTAL REHEARSAL

Visualization activates much of the same motor cortex activity as physical practice. 10–15 min daily — morning after waking or evening before sleep. Full race visualization should match real race time.

  • Both first-person AND third-person perspectives
  • Include sensory detail: chlorine, water feel, starter signal
  • Visualize the process more than the outcome
  • Also visualize imperfect scenarios and recovery

OPTIMAL AROUSAL

Too calm = under-activated. Too anxious = overthinking. Every swimmer has their own optimal zone — finding it is a training task.

REGULATION TOOLS

DOWN-regulation: Box breathing 5×, progressive muscle relaxation, slow warm-up, 60–80 bpm music.

UP-regulation: Explosive warm-up bursts, 120–140 bpm music, explosive activation (arm swings, 3–5 jump squats), activating self-talk.

FOCUS CUES

  • Maximum 2 focus cues per race
  • Cues should be action-based: "High elbow" not "Don't drop my elbow"
  • Practice cues in training until automatic
  • Self-talk in final 50 m: "This is what you train for."

PRE-RACE ROUTINE

  • T−15 min: 3 min box breathing
  • T−12 min: Visualize first 50 m in real time
  • T−8 min: Review 2 focus cues only
  • T−5 min: Physical activation behind the blocks — arm swings, fast squats
  • T−1 min: One breath. Step up on the long whistle and activate.

Stay in the Water

INJURY PREVENTION

Swimmer's Shoulder is the most common swimming injury. 60–80% of elite swimmers experience it. Most cases are preventable.

CAUSES

  • Serratus anterior weakness
  • Rotator cuff imbalance
  • Crossover entry (crossing centerline)
  • Flat, dropped elbow catch
  • Excessive paddle volume

PREVENTION

  • Serratus punches: 2×15 daily (3×15 on dryland days)
  • External rotation: sidelying, 1×15 each side daily, 3×15 on dryland days
  • Correct entry: never cross centerline
  • High elbow catch on every single stroke

BREASTSTROKER'S KNEE

  • Primary cause: valgus/rotation load from a wide-knee recovery and kick volume; hip abductor/adductor weakness adds to it
  • Copenhagen plank: 3×20 s daily (in the Prevention routine; also on Dryland Day 2)
  • Narrow heel recovery drill
  • Self-test: kick on your back — can you see the inside of your knees?

LOW BACK PAIN

  • Cause: tight hip flexors + weak glutes + weak deep core
  • Daily hip flexor stretching (90/90, couch stretch)
  • Glute activation: hip thrusts, clamshells
  • Dead bug for deep core stability
Hip impingement and flexor tightness show up most in breaststroke and butterfly swimmers, where the hip moves through a large range under load every single cycle.

CAUSES

  • Repetitive breaststroke whip-kick external rotation under load
  • Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) from years of forced hip ROM
  • Chronically tight hip flexors from the prone swimming position
  • Weak glute medius — lets the hip drop or rotate during the kick
  • Butterfly's repeated hip-driven undulation without adequate mobility work

PREVENTION

  • 90/90 hip switches: 1 min daily (in the Prevention routine; also in the Dryland mobility routine) — builds rotational range in both directions
  • Glute medius activation: banded clamshells, monster walks, 3×/week
  • Couch stretch for hip flexor length, 2 min per side daily
  • Limit breaststroke whip-kick volume during growth spurts in younger swimmers
  • Any pinching pain at the front of the hip during kick — stop and get it assessed, don't swim through it

RETURN TO KICK AFTER HIP PAIN

  • Phase 1: Flutter or dolphin kick only — no breaststroke whip-kick
  • Phase 2: Slow, shallow-range breaststroke kick on the back, focused on pain-free range only
  • Phase 3: Full-range breaststroke kick again — start at 50% of your normal kick volume for the first week back, then build

SWIMMER'S ELBOW (MEDIAL/LATERAL EPICONDYLITIS)

Less common than shoulder issues but shows up in swimmers who paddle-train heavily or have a flat, dropped-elbow catch that loads the forearm flexors and extensors instead of the lat and pec.

  • Primary cause: excessive paddle volume before the forearm has built tolerance
  • Secondary cause: a catch that relies on wrist/forearm flexion instead of a high elbow — the small muscles around the elbow take over for the big back muscles
  • Prevention: introduce paddles gradually over weeks, not days; fix the high-elbow catch first so the lat does the work
  • Forearm work: wrist curls and reverse wrist curls, 3×12, two or three times a week if you paddle-train, to build tendon tolerance — an add-on, not a slot in the Dryland plan
  • Self-test: pain when gripping or extending the wrist against resistance points to the elbow, not the shoulder

NECK PAIN — BREATHING AND HEAD POSITION

Neck pain in swimmers is almost always a head-position problem, not a neck-strength problem. Habitually lifting the head to breathe instead of rotating it within the body's bow wave puts repetitive strain on the cervical spine across thousands of strokes per week.

  • Freestyle: lifting the head to breathe instead of rotating with the body roll — fix the stroke before treating the symptom
  • Backstroke: a chronic chin-tuck (looking at the feet) or a lifted chin — keep the neck neutral, the Duck-drill position (Technique › Backstroke)
  • Breaststroke: lifting the chin to breathe instead of riding the body's natural lift from the pull
  • Prevention: daily neck CARs (controlled articular rotations), and film yourself breathing from above to check for head lift
  • Persistent neck pain unrelated to a recent technique change warrants a look from a physio rather than continued self-treatment
Breathing issues in swimmers are common and often under-discussed. Chlorine exposure, breath-holding training, and the unique mechanics of swim breathing all play a role.

EXERCISE-INDUCED BRONCHOCONSTRICTION (EIB)

  • Significantly more common in competitive swimmers than the general athletic population, likely linked to chronic low-level chlorine byproduct exposure in indoor pools
  • Symptoms: coughing, wheezing, or chest tightness during or after hard sets — easy to mistake for "just being out of breath"
  • If symptoms appear consistently with intensity (not just once), get evaluated by a doctor rather than assuming it's normal fatigue
  • Good pool ventilation and showering promptly after practice reduce chlorine byproduct exposure

UNDERWATER WORK & BREATH-HOLDING SAFETY

  • Shallow water blackout is a real risk from repeated breath-holding or hyperventilation before underwater swimming — it can happen with no warning symptoms
  • Never practice extended underwater distance alone, and never hyperventilate before a long underwater swim to "load up on oxygen"
  • UW training sets (15 m off the wall) at normal training volumes are not the same risk category as deliberate breath-hold/static apnea training — the latter needs supervision and should not be self-coached from generic advice
  • Coaches should know the difference between healthy UW kick technique work and breath-hold training that carries real risk

GENERAL RESPIRATORY HEALTH FOR SWIMMERS

  • A persistent cough, recurring chest tightness, or breathing that feels disproportionately hard relative to your fitness level are reasons to see a doctor, not push through
  • Asthma is manageable for competitive swimmers with proper diagnosis and treatment — many elite swimmers compete successfully with diagnosed asthma
  • This section is informational only and does not replace medical evaluation for breathing symptoms
Load management is #1. A common heuristic is to add no more than about 10% of volume per week — a rule of thumb from running, not swim-tested evidence — with a lighter week every 3–4 and no sudden jumps after time off.

  • Serratus punches: 2×15
  • External rotation: 1×15 each side

    SWOLF, STROKE RATE & CRITICAL SWIM SPEED

    Three deck-side calculations that turn a stopwatch and a stroke count into numbers you can track over a season. They work with or without log data.

    SWOLF — ONE LENGTH

    —
    SWOLF

    HOW TO READ IT

    SWOLF = seconds for one length + strokes for that length. Lower is better. It rewards covering the length faster and with fewer strokes, so it tracks efficiency rather than speed alone.
    • Pool length matters. A 50 m SWOLF is roughly double a 25 m one for the same swimmer, and a 25 m pool has a push-off and glide every length that counts for no strokes. Only compare scores from the same pool length, from the same kind of start (push, not dive).
    • Counting convention. Freestyle and backstroke: count every hand entry (each arm). Butterfly and breaststroke: one stroke per cycle. Use the same convention every time.
    • Same effort, same pool, over weeks. The useful signal is your own trend at a fixed pace or effort — for example, best SWOLF on a 25 at 200-pace. Comparing against other swimmers is confounded by height and arm span.
    • Relation to Golf Score. The Golf Score on the Calculator page is the same idea over 50 m (50 m split + strokes per 50) and has its own level guide there.

    CRITICAL SWIM SPEED (400 / 200)

    —
    CSS pace per 100
    ReferencePer 50Per 100Per 200Per 400

    METHOD & ASSUMPTIONS

    CSS = (400 − 200) ÷ (T400 − T200). It is the slope of the distance–time line through two maximal swims — the two-point field version of the critical velocity test described by Wakayoshi and colleagues (1992).
    • Both swims all-out, from a push, in the same pool, well rested. A common field protocol is the 400 first, then full recovery (or a separate session), then the 200. A soft 400 or a 200 swum tired makes CSS wrong in opposite directions.
    • What it approximates. Critical velocity is defined as the speed that could in theory be held without exhaustion; in practice it correlates strongly with 400 m performance and sits close to the 4 mmol/L lactate speed (Wakayoshi et al. 1992, 1993). Later work found it slightly overestimates the pace at maximal lactate steady state (Dekerle et al. 2005), so repeats "at CSS" are demanding threshold work, not comfortable aerobic swimming.
    • The table below the result lists your own 400 and 200 paces beside CSS for comparison. How to train around CSS — offsets, repeat distance, rest — is your coach's call; this page does not prescribe it.
    • D′ (anaerobic distance capacity) is the line's intercept: roughly the distance you can cover above CSS before fatigue forces you back down. From only two swims it is a rough estimate — read it in tens of meters, not units.
